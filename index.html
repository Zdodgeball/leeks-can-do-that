Leeks Can Do That

The leek has been busy.

Eight things this long, quiet vegetable has done for gardens, kitchens and emperors.

See all eight

Survive the winter.

Leeks are among the toughest vegetables in the garden. Hardy varieties stand through hard frosts, so you can pull them fresh in the cold months when little else is growing.

Stand for a nation.

The leek is a national emblem of Wales, worn on St David's Day, 1 March. By legend, Welsh soldiers wore leeks on their caps to tell friend from foe in battle against the Saxons.

Steady an emperor's voice.

Roman writers report that Nero ate leeks to clear his singing voice, and gave him a nickname for it: porrophagus, the leek eater.

Make a soup famous.

Vichyssoise, a chilled leek and potato soup, was popularized by chef Louis Diat at the Ritz-Carlton in New York around 1917. He based it on the hot version he grew up eating in France.

Feed your gut bacteria.

Leeks contain inulin, a fiber that passes through digestion untouched and is fermented by bacteria in your large intestine. That makes it a prebiotic.

Turn scraps into stock.

The dark green tops are too tough to eat but full of flavor. Simmer them with the root-end trimmings in water, then strain, and you have a stock for soups and risotto.

Feed the bees.

Leeks flower in their second year. Leave one in the ground and it sends up a round, pom-pom head of tiny blooms that pollinators visit.

Grow tall and pale.

Pile soil around the stems as they grow, or plant them in a deep trench. With the light shut out, the lower stem stays white and tender, and gets long.

Rinse between the layers.

Leeks trap grit as they grow. Slice one lengthwise, fan the layers open under running water, then chop. Your soup won't crunch.
